About this map

Cyber Attack Earth shows every cyber attack that somebody wrote down. Each missile is one publicly documented incident, flying from where it was attributed to where it landed, from the year 2000 to today. Press play and two decades pass a month at a time.

It draws on roughly 37,000 incidents from five independent sources — the European Repository of Cyber Incidents, the VERIS Community Database, ransomware leak sites, an academic events database and a hand-curated set with primary references — alongside vulnerability and industrial-advisory data used for context. Everything is rebuilt nightly and published as an open data pack.

What it is for. Three questions it answers better than a table: has this kind of attack actually grown, or has recording simply improved; who is being hit, and does that change once you adjust for population; and how much of the picture rests on attribution that nobody has actually made.

What it is not. It is not a live threat feed, not a risk score, and not a picture of what is happening now. It records what was disclosed, which is a small and uneven fraction of what occurred. The Compare window tests it against published findings from ENISA, Verizon, Dragos and EuRepoC, including where they disagree.

Built as a personal project from public data. No trackers, no analytics, no advertising. Nothing here represents the views of any employer or organisation.

Reading severity — the most important caveat

In this dataset, Critical severity can only originate from EuRepoC or a small hand-curated layer. The VERIS Community Database caps at High, and ransomware leak-site claims are coded Medium unless the victim was widely reported.

EuRepoC's fully expert-reviewed release covers incidents up to 31 December 2024. Anything after that date comes from their live TableView working data, which EuRepoC state is not fully expert-reviewed — and most of those records are still marked as being coded rather than finished. Recent months are also dominated by ransomware leak-site claims.

So any apparent change in Critical and High severity after 2024 reflects coding lag, coding status and source composition. It should not be read as a change in real-world severity.

The Where the data comes from, by year chart shows this composition directly, and every incident card states which layer and coding status it came from.

Other limitations
  • These are documented incidents, not all attacks. The overwhelming majority are never publicly disclosed or catalogued.
  • Growth partly reflects better recording. Disclosure regimes, breach notification laws and dataset coverage all expanded over this period. Some of the rise is real; some is improved measurement. The two cannot be cleanly separated.
  • Coverage is uneven by geography. Countries with mandatory disclosure and active research communities appear far more often. A sparse region means sparse reporting, not safety.
  • Attacker origins are usually proxies. Where a country is shown as an origin it generally reflects public attribution, an indictment, sanctions or an ecosystem association — not a verified launch location.
  • Leak-site claims are unverified. They are the attacker's own account, and some claimed victims dispute them.
  • Vulnerability counts are context, not attacks. The CVE and KEV series measure attack surface and known exploitation, not incidents.
Why the total is “records”, not “attacks”

Several independent projects catalogue cyber incidents, and they overlap. One attack on one company can appear in three of them, under three slightly different names, with three different dates — the day it started, the day it was noticed, the day it was announced.

This tool tries to spot those and merge them into one record, matching on the victim, the date and the country. It catches most, but it will not catch all: “St Mary’s NHS Trust” and “St Marys Hospital” look like two victims to a computer. So read the headline figure as the number of records held, not as a count of distinct events. The real number of documented attacks is somewhat lower; the number of actual attacks is very much higher.

This is why more sources would not automatically be better. Every feed added brings its own version of events already in here, and an attack counted twice is worse than an attack not counted at all — because the error is invisible and it inflates every total, every league table and every trend line built on top of it. A new source earns its place by being checkable, licensable and de-duplicable against what is already here, not by adding volume.

The Evidence filter in Controls is the tool for this. It separates what has been reviewed by researchers from what is a single report or an attacker’s own claim, so you can see how much of any pattern rests on the firmest ground. If a finding only holds when the weakest tier is included, that is worth knowing before you repeat it.

Methodology

Records are normalised to a common incident schema and deduplicated across sources using date, victim country and a suffix-normalised victim-name key. Ransomware.live records are leak-site claims — attacker assertions, placed at country level, with no origin asserted. Unknown attacker locations launch from a neutral ocean anchor. Incident actors are matched against a threat-group directory built from an embedded core plus MITRE ATT&CK intrusion sets and Ransomware.live group profiles, each cited with links.
